How the pool works

  • Bodies are grown, not designed. A genome is a list of genes; each adds a cell of one of nine types next to an existing cell. Genes can be duplicated, deleted, inserted or repurposed, so bodies can grow without limit (up to 64 cells for now).
  • Cells specialise: photo (light to energy), mouth (eat dead matter or other creatures), flagellum (swim), eye (see further), armour, store (energy reserve), neuron (a bigger brain), gonad (sex), skin.
  • Three habitats along a coast. Reefs split Darwin's bay into the open sea (dimmer, richer water from deep upwellings, steady temperature), the reef shallows, and the tidal flats, where the tide comes in and out every three days: creatures left high and dry move slowly and lose water, but get full sun. Drifting straits in the reefs let life move between them, so a crash in one habitat no longer ends life everywhere. Zoom out to see the coast, land (no life yet) and sky (no flyers yet).
  • Mammal-like traits can evolve: warm blood (a creature holds its own temperature, so cold and heat hurt it far less, at the price of half again as much food) and parental care (parents keep feeding their young while they stay close). Both start at zero and spread only if they pay.
  • A real food web. Light feeds producers, corpses feed grazers, and any mouth can attack: success depends on attack against defence, and bigger bodies are harder to eat.
  • Bigger bodies run cheaper per cell (metabolism scales with size to the ¾ power, as in living things) but cost more to build.
  • The world keeps changing on many clocks: day and night, seasons, a drifting sunlit band, temperature, ocean current and upwellings, slow orbital cycles and rare catastrophes.
  • Waste and corpses fertilise the water, so what lives in a place changes what can live there next.
  • The clock is declared, not measured: 1 generation = {{ years_per_gen }} years, so a billion years is a million generations. At the current speed that is about six weeks of running.
