Barricelli's Symbiosis

In 1953 the mathematician Nils Aall Barricelli used John von Neumann's computer at night to breed numbers. A ring of cells, a few integers, three rules, and no design. What came out reproduced, cooperated, parasitised, crossed and repaired itself. This is a live reconstruction: every run starts from random numbers, and every organism named below was found, not written. How it works, slowed down ↓

Symbioorganisms

What just happened

The three rules

  1. Move. A gene n in cell a copies itself to cell a + n in the next generation. +3 jumps three cells right; −2 two cells left.
  2. Multiply by meeting. If the cell it lands on already holds a gene m, it copies itself again, to a + m — and again if that cell is full too. A gene alone only drifts. Genes that keep landing on each other multiply. That is the whole engine of cooperation.
  3. Collide. When different genes arrive in one cell, a norm decides: annihilate (Barricelli's norm 0), fuse into their sum, or produce a random new gene.

A symbioorganism here is a set of genes that only multiply by using each other, in a closed loop. A parasite uses an organism's genes but gives nothing back. A crossing is a new organism built from genes of two others. Self-repair is an organism that loses more than half its copies and grows back.

What Barricelli found, and what this is

Working at night on the IAS machine (512 cells of memory for his universe, 5 KB in all), he saw self-reproducing groups of numbers appear on their own, along with parasites, crossing between organisms and repair after damage. He took it as support for symbiogenesis: the idea that complexity comes from organisms merging, not only from mutation. He also hit a wall: one simple organism tended to take over the whole universe and evolution stalled. His remedies were different norms in different regions and swapping segments between parallel universes. Both are switches above.

This page follows his published shift, replication and collision norms; details such as the chain limit, the gene range and the exact mixture of norms are reconstructions. Organisms are detected from who-multiplies-by-whom over the last 12 generations.

Does it help the tidal pool?

It already has: Barricelli's takeover problem is the one the pool hit this week, and his remedy, a divided world, is how the pool's basins and habitats were built. It does not send creatures to the pool (the two worlds are made of different stuff), but it is a fast laboratory for the one thing the pool cannot do yet: separate lineages merging into one organism. What makes merging pay here is the best guide to adding it there.

3 · Collisions: the three norms

A +2 jumps right two cells and a −3 jumps left three. Both land in the same cell. What happens next is the collision norm, the one rule Barricelli varied most.

Norm 0 · annihilate

Barricelli's basic rule. Two different genes can't share a cell, so both are lost. Competition is absolute: one niche, one occupant.

Sum · fuse

The two fuse into one gene, their sum (if it stays within range). Material is merged rather than destroyed, so new genes are built from old ones.

Random · mutate

Half the time a random new gene appears; otherwise the cell stays empty. This is mutation: novelty with no plan. Replay to roll again.

4 · How an organism is recognised

Each circle is a gene value and each arrow means "multiplies by landing on". A and B are closed loops: every member is copied with another member's help, so the group copies itself as a whole. +6 only takes. Remove B and +6 stops multiplying; remove +6 and B doesn't notice.

Who uses whom

Every time a gene n earns an extra copy by landing on gene m, the page records a link n → m (each dot travelling along an arrow is one such copy). Over the last 12 generations the links form a network.

  • Symbioorganism: genes that use each other in a closed loop, in both directions and often. A loop can be two genes (A: +3 ⇄ −2) or longer (B: +5 → −4 → +1 → +5). Formally these are the network's strongly connected components, found with Tarjan's algorithm.
  • Parasite: a gene that multiplies by landing on an organism's genes while the organism almost never lands on it (+6). It takes copies and gives none back.
  • Crossing: a new loop that shares genes with two living organisms, i.e. built from parts of both.
  • Self-repair: an organism that loses over half its copies and grows back to near its former size.

Weak links are ignored: a link counts only if it fired at least max(8, 0.4% of all links) times in the window. An organism gets a name only after it has lasted 20 generations with at least 6 genes. Barricelli did all this by eye, reading printouts.

5 · Takeover, and the first remedy: different rules in different places

The same random start, run twice. Watch the green trace (number of different genes) at the top right of each panel.

With one rule everywhere, the first organism that copies itself efficiently fills the ring and everything else dies. Barricelli called it organized homogeneity: a living universe where evolution has stopped. Splitting the ring into regions with different collision norms stops any one organism being best everywhere. Each region favours different survivors, and they keep leaking into each other's territory.

6 · The second remedy: parallel universe exchange

Two universes evolve side by side. Every 50 generations a random stretch of one eighth of the ring (here 16 of 128 cells) is swapped between them at the same positions (violet flash). The third panel is universe 1 again, with no exchange.

Each universe drifts toward its own winners. A swapped stretch drops organisms into a world they did not evolve in. Most die; some find partners and form crossings no single universe would produce. The exchange keeps reintroducing variety that local winners would otherwise erase. In this run the control collapses to two genes at around generation 700 and never recovers, while both exchanging universes keep 9 to 15. Mixed norms alone usually hold out; the exchange is insurance against the runs where they don't.

In the main simulation, the "parallel universe exchange" switch does exactly this: a second, invisible universe with the same settings, and every 50 generations a random stretch of N/8 cells (64 of 512) is swapped. Only universe 1 is drawn. Biologists know this as the island model: populations kept mostly apart, with occasional migrants, preserve more diversity than one well-mixed population, and the same trick drives "island" genetic algorithms in computing.

Is this how nature does it?

Partly, and the parts that match are the interesting ones. Barricelli's bet was that life's big steps came from cooperation and merger, not only from mutation. Biology has since found real versions of nearly every phenomenon on this page.

Cooperating copiers: the hypercycle

In 1977 Manfred Eigen and Peter Schuster proposed the hypercycle: a loop of self-copying molecules in which each one speeds up the copying of the next. It is the chemical form of a symbioorganism. Each member multiplies only through the others, as +3 and −2 do in animation 2.

Its known weakness is Barricelli's parasite: a molecule that takes help from the loop and gives none back can drain it (dashed violet). In 1991 Maarten Boerlijst and Paulien Hogeweg showed the cure is space: on a grid, hypercycles form rotating spirals that keep parasites at the edges. That is the same lesson as the mixed-norm regions above. Structure in space protects cooperation.

Organisms that merged: symbiogenesis

The idea that new organisms arise by merging, symbiogenesis, was proposed by Konstantin Mereschkowski in 1905. Barricelli named his experiments after it. Lynn Margulis made the case for it in 1967, and it is now settled for two of the most important events in the history of life. Mitochondria descend from free-living bacteria (related to today's alphaproteobacteria) taken up by another cell well over a billion years ago, and chloroplasts descend from cyanobacteria. Every animal and plant cell is a crossing in Barricelli's sense.

Where the analogy breaks

  • A gene here is just a number with nothing to do but get copied. Nature's replicators build bodies (phenotypes) that must survive an environment. Barricelli saw this gap, and in later work had his number-organisms play a simple board game (Tac Tix), so that skill rather than mere copying could decide who spread.
  • The norms are chosen by the experimenter. In nature they are fixed by chemistry and physics and nobody picks them.
  • The universe is a ring of a few hundred cells. Real populations are vast, which changes how much chance and selection each matter.
  • Nothing here has an inside: no membrane separates an organism from its neighbours. Real cooperation became stable once cells enclosed their partners.

What it means for the tidal pool

The tidal pool hit Barricelli's wall: armoured monocultures that took over and stalled. Its fixes follow his remedies and their biological versions. Basins and habitats are its "mixed norms". Narrow straits between basins are its "parallel universe exchange". Since Oct 27, evolving pathogens that hunt the commonest genotype supply the parasites, which is the pressure that makes sex worth having. What the pool still lacks is the event this page is named for: two separate lineages merging into one organism.
